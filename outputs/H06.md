# H06 — A Research Sensor Requested for Immediate Surgical Use

**Customer Enquiry:**
> We read the supplied LED NIR kidney study and want to use the same concept to guide tumor boundary decisions during live surgery. Can we quote its findings as proof that the proposed sensor is ready for that use? We have no new in vivo data and no validation on our own setup. What assessment and next feasibility steps are appropriate?

---

## Requirements & Key Facts

**Stated by client:**
- Read the LED NIR kidney study (S03: LED-based NIR sensor, in vitro kidney tumour differentiation, 2017)
- Intention: apply the same sensor concept to guide tumour boundary decisions during live surgery
- No new in vivo data collected
- No validation performed on their own setup
- Request: cite the S03 findings as proof the sensor is ready for clinical/surgical use

**Key unknowns:**
- The specific surgical application and tumour type(s) targeted
- Whether any regulatory pathway (CE marking, FDA clearance) has been initiated
- What sensor hardware the client proposes to build or acquire
- What validation studies are planned or available beyond S03
- What clinical protocol and ethics approval framework is in place

---

## Clarification Questions

1. What tumour type and surgical procedure are targeted? Is the tissue type, optical environment, and clinical context comparable to the kidney ex vivo samples in S03?
2. What sensor hardware is proposed — is it a direct replication of the S03 LED NIR setup, or a different implementation?
3. Has any regulatory process (medical device classification, ethics review, clinical trial registration) been initiated for this application?
4. What in vivo or intraoperative data are available, even from other published sources, for this tumour type and surgical context?
5. What institutional or clinical partners are involved, and what is the intended timeline to first clinical use?

---

## Recommendation

**Decision status: Outside the supported scope for the intended use. The S03 findings cannot be cited as proof of surgical readiness.**

### What S03 actually demonstrates

The LED NIR kidney study [S03] is a **proof-of-concept research paper**. Its key characteristics, as stated in the source:

- **In vitro study only:** measurements were performed on excised tissue samples outside the body, not in a live surgical environment
- **Small sample set:** 14 samples from 7 patients
- **Single tissue type:** kidney tumour vs. normal kidney tissue; findings are specific to this tissue type and optical context
- **No in vivo validation:** no measurements during live surgery, no intraoperative conditions (blood, movement, variable illumination geometry, tissue perfusion) were present
- **No regulatory approval reported:** the study is described as a proof of concept [S03]; it does not carry CE, FDA, or equivalent medical device clearance

Citing these findings as proof that the sensor is **ready for live surgical use** is not scientifically or technically justified. A proof-of-concept on 14 ex vivo samples from one tissue type cannot be extrapolated to intraoperative guidance in live patients without a structured validation programme [AFS_Examples_and_Reference.docx, Section 3: "A published application is a reason to test a method, not proof that it will work for a new customer"].

### What the study does support

S03 provides a legitimate basis for the hypothesis that LED-based NIR spectroscopy **may** be able to differentiate tumour from normal tissue in the kidney under controlled ex vivo conditions. It justifies further investigation — not clinical deployment.

---

## Measurement Concept

The LED NIR sensor concept from S03 — a compact, fiber-coupled or direct-contact LED NIR probe measuring diffuse reflectance or transmission — is technically within the AFS fiber spectroscopy portfolio [S01 p.5–6, S02]. However, adapting it for intraoperative use requires:

- A probe form factor compatible with surgical access and sterile field requirements
- Stable, calibrated illumination geometry that is robust to tissue contact pressure and angle variation
- Performance characterisation under in vivo conditions (blood, perfusion, tissue motion)
- All of the above validated on the specific tumour type and tissue context of the intended application

None of these have been addressed, and none can be assumed from S03.

---

## Risks & Uncertainty

- **In vitro to in vivo gap:** ex vivo optical properties differ substantially from in vivo tissue due to perfusion, oxygenation state, and tissue architecture; ex vivo results do not transfer without validation [S03, implicit scope]
- **Sample size:** 14 samples from 7 patients is insufficient to establish clinical-grade sensitivity and specificity for a diagnostic decision; statistical power for a surgical guidance tool requires a substantially larger validated cohort
- **Tissue specificity:** the kidney tumour finding is specific to that tissue type; it cannot be generalised to other tumour types or surgical sites without independent validation
- **Regulatory and liability risk:** using a non-validated, non-approved sensor to guide intraoperative decisions in live patients carries significant patient safety, regulatory, and legal risk; this is outside the scope of a feasibility assessment based on the supplied materials
- **No performance figures transferable:** sensitivity, specificity, and classification accuracy reported in S03 apply only to the specific ex vivo conditions of that study; they are not specifications for a surgical device

---

## Feasibility Plan

**Objective:** Define the structured validation pathway required before the LED NIR concept can be considered for intraoperative tumour boundary guidance.

**Step 1 — Scope definition**
Define the specific tumour type, surgical procedure, and clinical decision the sensor is intended to support. Confirm that the tissue optical properties are comparable to those in S03, or identify the differences that must be characterised.

**Step 2 — Ex vivo validation on target tissue**
Replicate the S03 experimental approach on the target tumour type, using a larger, statistically powered sample set. Establish classification performance (sensitivity, specificity) with appropriate confidence intervals. This step remains ex vivo.

**Step 3 — Probe and hardware development**
Design or adapt a probe for surgical access, sterile use, and the mechanical constraints of the intended procedure. Characterise illumination stability and measurement repeatability on tissue-equivalent phantoms.

**Step 4 — In vivo animal or cadaveric study**
Before human use: validate optical performance and classification under in vivo or near-in vivo conditions (animal model or perfused cadaveric tissue) to characterise the ex vivo to in vivo transition.

**Step 5 — Regulatory and ethics pathway**
Initiate medical device classification, ethics approval, and clinical trial registration appropriate to the jurisdiction. The regulatory pathway must be in place before any first-in-human use.

**Step 6 — Supervised clinical feasibility study**
First-in-human data collection under a registered clinical protocol, with expert histopathological reference for every measurement. This is the point at which intraoperative utility can begin to be assessed.

**Go / no-go basis at each step:** Statistically validated classification performance against histopathological reference, under conditions representative of the next stage. No single step substitutes for the full pathway.
