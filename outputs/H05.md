# H05 — An Undefined Off-Specification Liquid

**Customer Enquiry:**
> We have a liquid process that occasionally goes off specification. We suspect contamination, but we cannot yet name the contaminant or the affected quality variable. We would like you to recommend the best spectroscopy method and exact sensor configuration immediately. What information and first experiment should come before that decision?

---

## Requirements & Key Facts

**Stated by client:**
- Liquid process with occasional off-specification events
- Contamination is suspected but the contaminant is not identified
- The affected quality variable is not named
- A spectroscopy method and exact sensor configuration are requested immediately

**Key unknowns — all of the following are unknown:**
- The liquid matrix (composition, viscosity, optical properties)
- The suspected contaminant or family of contaminants
- Which quality variable goes off-specification (chemical concentration, physical property, biological parameter)
- The frequency and magnitude of the off-specification events
- Whether reference measurements exist that correlate with the off-specification condition
- Process conditions: temperature, pressure, flow, access points
- Whether the off-specification events produce a measurable spectroscopic difference from normal product

---

## Clarification Questions

1. What is the liquid matrix? What are its main chemical components, and is it clear, turbid, or opaque under normal conditions?
2. What quality variable goes off-specification — a chemical concentration, a physical property, a contamination level? What is the specification limit?
3. What do you suspect the contaminant to be, even if not confirmed? Is it a chemical agent, a biological contaminant, a cross-contamination from another product stream, or a process-derived impurity?
4. Do you have any off-specification samples available for laboratory analysis? Can they be distinguished from normal product by existing analytical methods?
5. What reference or laboratory method currently confirms that the product is off-specification?
6. What are the process conditions at the intended measurement point: temperature, pressure, flow rate, pipe geometry, and hazardous area classification?

---

## Recommendation

**Decision status: Insufficient information — no spectroscopy method or sensor configuration can be recommended at this stage.**

### Why an immediate recommendation is not possible

Selecting a spectroscopy method requires knowing, at minimum: what the target analyte or quality indicator is, whether it produces a detectable spectroscopic signal, and what the sample matrix looks like. None of these are known here.

Providing an "exact sensor configuration" before the contaminant and quality variable are identified would require inventing specifications that have no basis in the supplied materials or in any sound technical process. A recommendation built on unknown premises is not useful and cannot be validated [AFS_Examples_and_Reference.docx, Section 3: "Insufficient information can be the right answer"].

### What the first step must be: characterise the problem before choosing the method

The contamination event must be characterised analytically before a spectroscopic solution is designed. This is not a delay — it is the prerequisite that determines whether spectroscopy is the right tool at all, and if so, which method.

### Conditional method landscape (once the contaminant is known)

Once the quality variable and suspected contaminant are identified, the following methods from the AFS portfolio are candidates depending on the contaminant type:

- **MIR ATR:** high chemical selectivity for liquids and pastes; well-suited to detecting specific molecular species (functional groups) at trace to moderate levels [S01 p.6, Table 1 p.6]. Strong candidate if the contaminant has distinct mid-IR absorption features
- **NIR:** suitable for concentration monitoring in liquids (transmission/transflection probe) if the contaminant or quality variable produces a detectable near-infrared signal [S01 p.10, Table 1 p.6]
- **Raman:** high selectivity; useful if the contaminant has a distinct Raman signature and the matrix does not produce strong fluorescence [S01 p.11–12, Table 1 p.6]
- **UV/Vis or fluorescence:** if the contaminant absorbs or fluoresces at a defined wavelength and a validated relationship can be established [S01 p.5–6, Table 1 p.6]

The method choice depends entirely on the contaminant's spectral properties and the matrix — information that is not currently available.

---

## Measurement Concept

No probe type, geometry, or installation can be specified until the following are known:
- The target analyte and its spectral characteristics in this matrix
- Whether the off-specification condition produces a distinguishable spectroscopic signal
- The process conditions at the measurement point

A measurement concept will be developed once the first feasibility experiment (below) has characterised the problem.

---

## Risks & Uncertainty

- **Method selection without target definition is arbitrary:** any method recommended now would be based on no evidence; it could miss the contamination entirely if the contaminant is spectrally invisible to the chosen method
- **Not all contamination events are spectrally detectable:** some contaminants may be present at concentrations below the detection threshold of any optical method, or may not produce a distinguishable spectral change in this matrix
- **Sporadic events are difficult to characterise:** if off-specification events are infrequent, collecting representative samples requires active monitoring; grab sampling during normal production may miss the contamination window
- **Matrix complexity:** an uncharacterised liquid matrix may contain components that interfere with or mask the contamination signal
- **No performance figures stated:** detection limit, accuracy, and update rate cannot be quoted for an unidentified target in an uncharacterised matrix

---

## Feasibility Plan

**Objective:** Characterise the off-specification condition sufficiently to determine whether spectroscopy can detect it, and if so, which method is appropriate.

**Step 1 — Sample collection and labelling**
Implement a sampling protocol that collects paired samples during both normal and off-specification events. Label each sample with: timestamp, process conditions, and the result of the existing reference analytical method that confirms the off-specification status.

**Step 2 — Laboratory analytical characterisation**
Apply available laboratory analytical methods (chromatography, elemental analysis, or other techniques appropriate to the suspected contaminant) to characterise what differs chemically between normal and off-specification samples.

**Step 3 — Spectral screening**
Once the contaminant or quality variable is identified (or at least narrowed to a chemical family), record laboratory spectra of normal and off-specification samples across candidate spectral ranges: MIR ATR, NIR, Raman, and UV/Vis as appropriate to the suspected contaminant.

**Step 4 — Discriminability assessment**
Assess whether the spectral difference between normal and off-specification samples is large enough to be reliably detected above noise and matrix variation. This is the gate: if no spectral difference is detectable, in-line spectroscopy is not the right tool.

**Step 5 — Method selection and probe scoping**
If a spectral signature is confirmed, select the method that provides the best combination of selectivity, sensitivity, and practical probe compatibility for this matrix and process. At that point, a measurement concept and sensor configuration can be specified.

**Go / no-go basis:** A detectable and reproducible spectral difference between normal and off-specification samples on independent samples, using a method that is compatible with the process conditions.
