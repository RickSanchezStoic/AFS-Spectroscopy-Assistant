# H02 — Composition Monitoring Across Two Dairy Streams

**Customer Enquiry:**
> Our dairy process handles both a relatively clear whey stream and an opaque milk concentrate. We want a pilot for composition monitoring, using laboratory protein and fat results where those variables are relevant. We can compare different measurement interfaces before choosing the installation. What first measurement concepts and comparison experiment would make sense for these two streams?

---

## Requirements & Key Facts

**Stated by client:**
- Two distinct streams: (1) a relatively clear whey stream, (2) an opaque milk concentrate
- Target variables: protein and fat (where relevant to each stream)
- Existing laboratory reference results for protein and fat are available
- Ability to compare measurement interfaces before committing to installation
- No fixed installation point, probe type, or geometry specified yet

**Key unknowns:**
- Composition ranges and production variation for each stream
- Temperature range and whether it varies significantly during measurement
- Flow rate, pipe/vessel geometry, and access points for each stream
- Cleaning regime (CIP) and chemical compatibility requirements
- Required accuracy, response time, and permissible measurement delay
- Whether fat and protein are both relevant for the whey stream (whey is typically low in fat)

---

## Clarification Questions

1. What are the expected protein and fat concentration ranges for each stream, and what reference assay provides the independent laboratory values?
2. How does temperature vary in each stream during normal production, and does the whey stream change turbidity under some operating conditions?
3. What are the pipe or vessel geometries, flow conditions, and available access points for each stream?
4. What is the cleaning and sanitisation regime, and what probe materials must be chemically compatible?
5. What accuracy and response time are required — specifically, what prediction error and update interval would satisfy process control needs?
6. Is fat a relevant monitoring target for the whey stream, or is protein the primary variable there?

## Recommendation

**Decision status: Provisional assessment — two streams require separate measurement concepts.**

### Stream 1: Opaque milk concentrate — NIR diffuse reflection
NIR diffuse reflection is directly supported for opaque or concentrated dairy products [S01 p.17]. The high scattering of the concentrate makes transmission or transflection geometries less suitable as a first choice; diffuse reflection interacts with the surface layer and is the established starting point for this product type [S01 Table 1 p.6, S01 p.17].

### Stream 2: Clear whey stream — NIR transmission or transflection
For a clearer liquid, a transmission or transflection probe geometry is more appropriate [S01 p.17–18, Table 1 p.6]. However, because the whey stream can become more turbid under some operating conditions [as noted in related scenario D03, AFS_Examples_and_Reference.docx], the measurement concept must account for this variability. A transflection probe offers a shorter, defined optical path and may handle moderate turbidity changes better than a long-path transmission cell.

### Why not a single interface for both streams?
The two streams differ fundamentally in optical behaviour: one is opaque and scattering, the other is relatively clear but variable. A geometry optimised for one will not be optimal for the other. The pilot's value is precisely in comparing interfaces on each stream independently before selecting the production installation.

---

## Measurement Concept

**Milk concentrate:**
- NIR diffuse reflection probe; contact or near-contact geometry; optical depth 0.5–1 mm [S01 Table 1 p.6]
- Test under representative laboratory conditions first before specifying the process installation
- Confirm probe materials, fouling control, and CIP compatibility with AFS

**Whey stream:**
- NIR transflection probe as primary candidate; defined short optical path (1–10 mm) [S01 Table 1 p.6]
- Include a turbidity variation test: collect spectra at both clear and turbid conditions to assess sensitivity to optical changes
- Transmission cuvette geometry as a laboratory reference point before moving to in-line probe

**Spectral range:** The general NIR range is 780–2500 nm [S01 p.10]. The instrument and fiber actual coverage must be confirmed; the useful bands for protein and fat will be identified from sample spectra, not assumed from the nominal range.

---

## Risks & Uncertainty

- **Temperature and texture effects (milk concentrate):** changes in temperature, fat crystallisation state, and homogenisation can alter the NIR signal independently of composition [S01 p.17]. These effects must be separated from composition variation in the calibration
- **Turbidity variation (whey stream):** increased turbidity shifts the effective optical path and can mimic composition changes; the measurement concept must be tested under both clear and turbid conditions before deployment [S01 p.17–18]
- **Independent reference results per variable:** fat and protein each require their own independent laboratory reference values; a single combined reference is not sufficient for building separate calibration models
- **Interface and fouling:** dairy environments require regular cleaning; probe materials and geometry must be rated for the cleaning regime — this has not been confirmed
- **No accuracy figures are stated:** prediction error depends on the actual spectra, composition range, and physical variation; no figures are quoted without experimental data

---

## Feasibility Plan

**Objective:** Identify the most suitable NIR probe geometry for each stream and confirm that protein (and fat, where relevant) can be tracked against laboratory reference values with acceptable prediction error.

**Step 1 — Sample collection**
Collect representative samples from each stream across the normal production variation: composition range, temperature range, and (for whey) turbidity range. Label each sample with independent laboratory protein and fat values.

**Step 2 — Laboratory spectral comparison**
Record NIR spectra from each stream using candidate geometries (diffuse reflection for concentrate; transflection and transmission for whey). Use the same spectrometer for both to allow direct comparison.

**Step 3 — Physical variation assessment**
For the whey stream: deliberately include samples at both low and high turbidity. For the milk concentrate: include samples at the temperature and texture extremes expected in production.

**Step 4 — Calibration and validation**
Build single-variable models (protein separately from fat) for each stream and geometry. Validate on held-out samples. Do not mix samples from the two streams in a single model.

**Step 5 — Interface comparison and selection**
Compare prediction error, bias, and robustness across geometries. Select the interface that meets the client's accuracy and maintenance requirements for each stream, independently.

**Go / no-go basis:** Agree numerical acceptance criteria (prediction error, bias) with the client before the experiment. The decision is made on independent production samples, not laboratory calibration blends.
